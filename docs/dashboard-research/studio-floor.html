Diagnosis

Three problems, in the order they have to be fixed

None of these is "it needs to be prettier". The visual problem is downstream of a modelling problem, and fixing the pixels first would just make a confident-looking wrong answer.

PROBLEM ONE

Trust comes before beauty

If a drag on the board does not reach ClickUp, people stop dragging and go back to Slack within a week. Nothing else on this page matters until a move on screen is a move in the system — and visibly says so.

→ Specimen J

PROBLEM TWO

It reports state; the job is distributing work

Pace races and rockets are scoreboards — read once a day, then closed. Today is a workbench, opened thirty times a day by one person with a decision to make: who takes this. Those need opposite layouts.

→ Specimens A, B, C

PROBLEM THREE

Everything competes for the same attention

Stale jobs, client waits, overloaded people and FYI noise all arrive at the same visual volume. When everything is bold, the eye picks by position, not importance — so the top-left item wins regardless of whether it should.

→ Specimens D, E, K

The rules the specimens obey

Twelve principles

Drawn partly from how the best operations tools behave and partly from constraints specific to a nine-person studio billing in days. Each one is visible in the mockups below.

Colour
Colour means state, never identity
You have twelve active clients. Twelve client colours is a colour wheel nobody memorises, and it burns the one channel that should scream. Clients get neutral text chips; colour is reserved for over capacity, stale, waiting, on track. This single rule does more visual work than any other on the page.
Never alone
Colour always ships with a word
Red-green is the most common colour-vision deficiency and your studio is nine people. Every state is a pill: dot and label. It also survives being photographed, projected, or printed for a client meeting.
Modelling
Separate booked from parked
A job sitting in with the client is on Kavindu's name but not in Kavindu's day. Show it as a hatched segment outside the solid fill so his real free capacity stays honest.
Unit
Days and half-days, because that's the currency
You quote in days, you sell retainers in days, you argue with clients in days. Hours are an implementation detail. A card reads 1.5d, never 12h.
Target
Always draw the line they should be on
Your pace race already does this and it is the best idea in the current build — a bar alone is a number, a bar with a target tick is a verdict. Carry the dashed "where you should be" mark onto every meter in Today.
Sort
Sort order is the answer
When Yami has a job to place, the person who should take it belongs at the top of the list. Default-sort the capacity rail by most-free. The interface answers before she reads.
Queue
Notifications are a queue to empty, not a stream to watch
A feed implies you scroll until bored. An inbox implies you finish. Group by severity, give every row exactly one action, and let it reach zero — the day it hits zero is the day people believe it.
Disclosure
Summary on the surface, detail one click down
The tile carries the signal; the drawer carries the noise. Nine people on one screen means roughly forty characters per person before it turns to mush — spend them on the name, the load and the one thing that is wrong.
Speed
Keyboard-first for the person who lives here
Yami will touch this interface more than everyone else combined. J K to move, 1–9 to assign to a person, E to clear. Drag is for visitors; keys are for staff.
Receipts
Every write shows its receipt
Optimistic on screen, confirmed against ClickUp, and a visible failure lane with retry. This is the direct fix for what Yami hit this morning, and it is a design problem as much as a sync problem.
Ambient
One layout is meant to be read from four metres
A studio has walls and a spare screen. The wall version is not the laptop version scaled up — bigger type, fewer objects, no interaction, and it must be true at a glance from the kitchen.
Empty
The empty state is the instruction manual
"Nothing needs you. 6 jobs land tomorrow." beats a shrugging illustration. Empty states are the most-read copy in any ops tool because the good days outnumber the bad ones.

Notifications

Four tiers, and only one of them may interrupt

ClickUp already fires a message into #new-project-to-work-on for every status change and every assignee edit — this morning alone that channel took roughly forty machine posts before lunch. Nobody reads it, which means the useful events in it are invisible too. The fix is not fewer notifications; it is ranked notifications, where each tier gets a different delivery route.

Tier 1 · Now
A client is blocked on us
Slack DM to the one person who can unblock it, plus a red row in the queue. Rough guide: no more than two of these a day. Example — Maptek have waited two days on the video sizing answer.
Tier 2 · Today
Money is leaking
Queue row, no interrupt. Work going stale past 10 days, a retainer with nothing booked, someone under half capacity for a third day. Read when the queue is opened, not when the event fires.
Tier 3 · 4:30pm
Everything else that has a consequence
Rolled into the digest (specimen K). Reassignments, jobs completing, tomorrow's shape. One message, one time, one place.
Tier 4 · Never
Machine chatter
Status transitions, assignee edits, task-created events. It belongs in the record, not in anyone's day. Turn the ClickUp Slack integration down to the events a human would actually mention out loud.

The number to watch: what share of notifications got acted on. Ops teams generally treat anything under about a third as a broken alerting system — below that, people stop reading, and you cannot tell the difference between a quiet day and an ignored one. Log every dismiss so you can actually measure this rather than guess.

The order I would do it in

This is a genuine sequence — each step is worth less if the one above it is missing. Steps 1 to 3 are the whole product; everything after is upside.

1

Specimen J. Until a drag reaches ClickUp and says so, nothing else is worth building — and Yami has already found the bug, so you have a test case waiting.

must · small

must · large

cheap · visible

Then the slow-burn views

Fairness (H), the promise strip (I), the day timeline (F). Monthly and quarterly instruments — real value, but none of them change what happens on a Wednesday.

later

One thing to decide before step 3. Does the board plan today or tomorrow? Yami's Slack post is written in the afternoon for the next day, so the studio's real planning horizon is tomorrow — but the tab is called Today. Pick one and name the tab after it. Trying to be both is how these screens end up ambiguous, and it is the single most likely reason a good build still gets ignored.
